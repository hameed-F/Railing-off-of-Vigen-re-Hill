Network security is the practice of protecting computers, networks and the information they carry from unauthorized access, misuse and disruption. Every time a person sends a message, signs in to an account or pays for something online, a long chain of systems must cooperate to keep that action private and trustworthy. At the heart of this chain lies cryptography, the science of hiding the meaning of a message so that only the intended reader can recover it.

Long before computers existed, people already needed secret writing. Military commanders, merchants and diplomats all sent letters that enemies and rivals would gladly have read. The earliest methods were simple. A sender could shift every letter of the alphabet by a fixed number of places, or rearrange the letters according to a pattern that only the receiver knew. These classical techniques are easy to describe, and that is exactly why they are so useful for learning. Each one shows a basic idea in a form that can be worked out with pencil and paper.

A substitution cipher replaces each letter with another letter or symbol. The best known example is the shift cipher, in which the whole alphabet slides along by a secret amount. Because there are only twenty six possible shifts, an attacker can simply try them all. A more careful design uses a different shift for each position of the message, controlled by a keyword that repeats. This is the idea behind the Vigenere cipher, which resisted casual attack for several centuries. Its weakness is the repeating keyword. Once an analyst discovers the length of the keyword, the message splits into several shift ciphers, and each of these falls quickly to counting letters.

A transposition cipher leaves the letters unchanged but moves them to new places. In the rail fence cipher the message is written in a zigzag across several rows and then read off row by row. The letters keep their usual frequencies, so the ciphertext still looks like English when the counts are examined, which tells the analyst that the cipher is a transposition. On its own it offers little protection, because the number of rows is small and every choice can be tested in a moment.

The Hill cipher takes a different approach. It treats a short block of letters as a vector of numbers and multiplies that vector by a secret square matrix, working modulo twenty six. A change in one letter of the block alters every letter of the output block, which hides the ordinary pattern of single letter frequencies. For the matrix to be usable it must have an inverse modulo twenty six, and that is the case only when its determinant shares no common factor with twenty six. The cipher is elegant, yet it is entirely linear. An attacker who knows enough pairs of plaintext and ciphertext can solve ordinary equations and recover the matrix.

Combining ciphers is a natural way to repair the faults of each one. A designer may first mix the letters of a block, then add a keyed shift that changes from place to place, and finally scramble the order of the result. Each stage covers a gap left by another. The block step removes the obvious letter counts, the repeating key step stops block patterns from repeating too neatly, and the final shuffle hides the position of the key period from a casual observer. Still, a chain of simple steps is only as strong as its weakest link, and a chain made only of linear steps stays linear.

The security of any cipher should never depend on keeping its design secret. This principle, usually credited to Auguste Kerckhoffs, says that a system must remain safe even if everything about it except the key becomes public knowledge. Modern standards follow this rule closely. The algorithms are published, studied by thousands of experts and attacked for years before anyone trusts them with real secrets. Classical ciphers fail this test, and that failure is the most important lesson they teach.

When an analyst studies an unknown ciphertext, the first tool is usually frequency analysis. In ordinary English the letter E appears far more often than any other, followed by T, A, O and N. A cipher that preserves these counts leaks its secret almost at once. A second useful measure is the index of coincidence, which gives the chance that two letters picked at random from a text are the same. English text scores near zero point zero six six, while perfectly random letters score close to zero point zero three eight. A value near the lower number suggests that simple counting will not help, and that the analyst must look for deeper structure.

If the analyst also knows part of the original message, the situation changes completely. Many real messages begin with a greeting, a date or a standard heading, so a few known words can be enough to expose the whole key. This is called a known plaintext attack, and it is the standard test of whether a cipher is more than a puzzle. A design that falls to a short stretch of known text offers little real protection, however clever it may look.
